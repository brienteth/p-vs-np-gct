## 1. Mathematical Setup & Rank Constraints

In a row-separated 4-layer homogeneous Algebraic Branching Program (ABP) for $\operatorname{perm}_4(X)$ with layer sizes $(4, 4, w, 4)$, rows 1 ($a = (x_{1,0}..x_{1,3})$) and 2 ($b = (x_{2,0}..x_{2,3})$) contract into a $4 \times 4$ quadratic matrix factorization problem:
$$M_1(a, b)_{4 \times 5} \, M_2(a, b)_{5 \times 4} = P(a, b),$$
where $P(a, b)_{j_0, j_3} = (1 - \delta_{j_0, j_3})(a_{c_1} b_{c_2} + a_{c_2} b_{c_1})$ with $\{j_0, c_1, j_3, c_2\} = \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$.
Decomposing $M_1 = A_1(a) + B_1(b)$ and $M_2 = A_2(a) + B_2(b)$ by bidegree gives:
1. $A_1(a) A_2(a) \equiv 0$,
2. $B_1(b) B_2(b) \equiv 0$,
3. $A_1(a) B_2(b) + B_1(b) A_2(a) \equiv P(a, b)$.

Define the generic matrix ranks over the function fields $\mathbb{F}(a)$ and $\mathbb{F}(b)$:
$$r_1 = \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}(a)}(A_1(a)), \quad s_1 = \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}(b)}(B_1(b)), \quad r_2 = \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}(a)}(A_2(a)), \quad s_2 = \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}(b)}(B_2(b)).$$

### Proposition 1.1 (Necessary Rank Constraints) [PROVEN]
Any solution over any field $\mathbb{F}$ of $\operatorname{char} \ne 2$ must satisfy:
1. $0 \le r_1, s_1, r_2, s_2 \le 4$ (matrix dimensions are $4 \times 5$ and $5 \times 4$).
2. $r_1 + r_2 \le 5$ (Sylvester's rank inequality: $\operatorname{im}(A_2(a)) \subseteq \ker(A_1(a)) \subseteq \mathbb{F}(a)^5$).
3. $s_1 + s_2 \le 5$ (Sylvester's rank inequality: $\operatorname{im}(B_2(b)) \subseteq \ker(B_1(b)) \subseteq \mathbb{F}(b)^5$).
4. $\min(r_1, s_2) + \min(s_1, r_2) \ge 4$ (since $\det P(a, a) = -48 a_0^2 a_1^2 a_2^2 a_3^2 \ne 0$, $\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}(a)}(P(a, a)) = 4$, and $\operatorname{rank}(X + Y) \le \operatorname{rank}(X) + \operatorname{rank}(Y)$ with $\operatorname{rank}(A_1 B_2) \le \min(r_1, s_2)$).
5. $r_1 + s_1 \ge 4$ and $r_2 + s_2 \ge 4$ (since $\operatorname{rank}(M_1(a, a)) \ge \operatorname{rank}(P(a, a)) = 4$).

*Proof.* Verified above. Symbolic check on $\det P(a, a) = -48 \prod a_i^2$ is exact over $\mathbb{Z}$. $\square$

## 3. Elimination of Regimes $k_{\min} = 0$ and $k_{\min} = 1$

### Theorem 3.1 (Elimination of Regime $k_{\min} = 0$, Orbits 1 & 2) [PROVEN]
No solution exists for any tuple in Orbit 1 or Orbit 2.

*Proof.*  
In every tuple of Orbit 1 and Orbit 2, either $\min(r_1, s_2) = 0$ or $\min(s_1, r_2) = 0$.
- If $\min(r_1, s_2) = 0$, then either $A_1 \equiv 0$ or $B_2 \equiv 0$, which forces $A_1(a) B_2(b) \equiv 0$.
- Equation (iii) then reduces to $P(a, b) = B_1(b)_{4 \times 5} A_2(a)_{5 \times 4}$.
- By the bidegree swap symmetry $P(a, b) = P(b, a)$, setting $a \leftrightarrow b$ gives $P(a, b) = \tilde{A}_1(a)_{4 \times 5} \tilde{B}_2(b)_{5 \times 4}$ with $\tilde{A}_1(a) = B_1(a)$ and $\tilde{B}_2(b) = A_2(b)$.
- For any such product of width 5, the $16 \times 16$ Nisan matrix $N_{01}(P)$ has rank at most 5:
  $$N_{01}(P)_{(j_0, c_1), (j_3, c_2)} = \sum_{k=1}^5 \tilde{A}_{1; j_0, k, c_1} \tilde{B}_{2; k, j_3, c_2} \implies \operatorname{rank}(N_{01}(P)) \le 5.$$
- However, $\operatorname{rank}(N_{01}(P)) = 6$ identically (Euler, Theorem 1 / Lemma C). Contradiction ($6 \le 5$ is impossible).
The identical argument holds when $\min(s_1, r_2) = 0$ by transpose symmetry. $\square$

### Theorem 3.2 (Elimination of Regime $k_{\min} = 1$, Orbits 3, 4, 5, 6, 7) [PROVEN]
No solution exists for any tuple in Orbits 3, 4, 5, 6, or 7.

*Proof.*  
By $a \leftrightarrow b$ swap and transpose symmetry, assume without loss of generality that $r_1 = \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{C}(a)}(A_1(a)) = 1$.  
The matrix $A_1(a) = \sum_{i=0}^3 a_i C_1^{(i)}$ is a $4 \times 5$ linear matrix of generic rank 1.  
By the classical theorem of Westwick (1972) and Atkinson (1973) on linear spaces of matrices of bounded rank, any pencil of rank at most 1 over an algebraically closed field belongs to one of two geometric types:

1. **Type-Col (Constant column space in $\mathbb{C}^5$):**  
   There exists a constant nonzero vector $v \in \mathbb{C}^5$ such that $A_1(a) = u(a) v^T$, where $u(a) \in \mathbb{C}^4$ is linear in $a$.  
   By applying a constant gauge transformation $g \in \mathrm{GL}_5(\mathbb{C})$ to the middle layer ($M_1 \mapsto M_1 g, M_2 \mapsto g^{-1} M_2$), we can rotate $v$ to the first standard basis vector $e_1 = (1, 0, 0, 0, 0)^T$.  
   In this gauge:
   - Columns $2, 3, 4, 5$ of $A_1(a)$ are identically zero.
   - The product $A_1(a) B_2(b) = A_{1, \cdot, 1}(a) B_{2, 1, \cdot}(b)$ is carried entirely by wire 1, contributing a matrix of rank at most 1 to the Nisan matrix $N_{01}$.
   - The remaining middle wires $2, 3, 4, 5$ carry no $A_1$ contribution, meaning the factorization structurally reduces to the wire-split regime $(1, 4)$ (or $(0, 5)$).
   - By Lemma B (Swapped-Kronecker Rank Floor), $\operatorname{rank}(M - K(B, V)) \ge 5$ for all nonzero $B, V$, whereas a single $(a, b)$-wire has rank at most 1. This directly contradicts $N_{01}(A_1 B_2) = M - K$. Hence Type-Col cannot solve the system.

2. **Type-Row (Constant row space in $\mathbb{C}^4$):**  
   There exists a constant nonzero vector $u \in \mathbb{C}^4$ such that $A_1(a) = u v(a)^T$, where $v(a) \in \mathbb{C}^5$ is linear in $a$.  
   Then $A_1(a) B_2(b) = u (v(a)^T B_2(b))$.  
   The $3$-dimensional orthogonal complement $u^\perp = \{ \gamma \in \mathbb{C}^4 : \gamma^T u = 0 \}$ satisfies $\gamma^T A_1(a) \equiv 0$ for all $\gamma \in u^\perp$.  
   Multiplying equation (iii) on the left by $\gamma^T$ yields:
   $$\gamma^T P(a, b) = \gamma^T A_1(a) B_2(b) + \gamma^T B_1(b) A_2(a) = (\gamma^T B_1(b)) A_2(a).$$
   Let $\Gamma \in \mathbb{C}^{3 \times 4}$ be any matrix whose rows form a basis of $u^\perp$. Then the restricted $3 \times 4$ quadratic matrix $P_{u^\perp}(a, b) := \Gamma P(a, b)$ satisfies the **pure bilinear factorization**:
   $$P_{u^\perp}(a, b) = W(b)_{3 \times 5} A_2(a)_{5 \times 4}, \quad \text{where } W(b) = \Gamma B_1(b).$$
   Consider the bilinear flattening $T$ of $P_{u^\perp}(a, b)$ with row index $(i, c_2) \in \{0, 1, 2\} \times \{0..3\}$ (size 12) and column index $(j_3, c_1) \in \{0..3\} \times \{0..3\}$ (size 16), defined by:
   $$T_{(i, c_2), (j_3, c_1)} = [a_{c_1} b_{c_2}] (P_{u^\perp})_{i, j_3} = \sum_{j_0=0}^3 \Gamma_{i, j_0} M_{(j_0, c_1), (j_3, c_2)} = ((\Gamma \otimes I_4) M)_{(i, c_2), (j_3, c_1)}.$$
   Because $P_{u^\perp}(a, b) = W(b)_{3 \times 5} A_2(a)_{5 \times 4}$, the matrix $T$ factors as $T = U_{12 \times 5} V_{5 \times 16}$ where $U_{(i, c_2), k} = W_{i, k, c_2}$ and $V_{k, (j_3, c_1)} = A_{2; k, j_3, c_1}$.  
   Consequently, the rank of $T$ cannot exceed the inner dimension 5:
   $$\operatorname{rank}(T) \le 5.$$
   However, by **Lemma C (Kernel-Complement Survival)** from `THEOREM_W5_EULER_PROOF.md`:
   $$\operatorname{rank}\big((\Gamma \otimes I_4) M\big) = 6 \quad \text{for every nonzero } u \in \mathbb{C}^4 \text{ and basis } \Gamma \text{ of } u^\perp.$$
   This yields $6 \le 5$, an immediate contradiction.  
   Therefore, Type-Row is mathematically impossible.

Combining both cases, no linear matrix of generic rank 1 can satisfy the system. By symmetry, the same holds for $s_1 = 1, r_2 = 1, s_2 = 1$.  
Thus, all 16 tuples in Orbits 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 are rigorously eliminated. $\square$

## 5. The Big Picture: What Settling $w = 5$ Means for VP vs VNP

### 5.1 The Model: Row-Separated Homogeneous ABPs
An Algebraic Branching Program (ABP) computing $\operatorname{perm}_n(X)$ is **row-separated** if its vertex layers partition the rows of $X$.  
In our $n = 4$ setting, the layers are:
- Layer 0: source (row 0 of $X$),
- Layer 1: reads linear combinations of rows 1 and 2 ($a$ and $b$),
- Layer 2: reads linear combinations of rows 1 and 2 ($a$ and $b$),
- Layer 3: sink (row 3 of $X$).

When adjacent layers read disjoint variables (e.g. Layer 1 reads row 1 only, Layer 2 reads row 2 only), **Nisan's Theorem (1991)** applies directly:
$$\text{Width at cut } \{0, 1\} \mid \{2, 3\} = \operatorname{rank}_{\text{Nisan}}(\operatorname{perm}_4) = \binom{4}{2} = 6.$$
Grenet's (1987) canonical construction achieves exactly this cut width:
$$\text{Layer widths } (1, 4, 6, 4, 1) \implies \text{Determinantal size } m = 1 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 15.$$

### 5.2 Why $w = 5$ Was Open: Adjacent-Layer Variable Overlap
When Layer 1 ($M_1$) and Layer 2 ($M_2$) are both permitted to read **both** row 1 ($a$) and row 2 ($b$), they can exploit commutative cancellations:
$$A_1(a) A_2(a) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad B_1(b) B_2(b) = 0.$$
This polynomial cancellation could conceivably allow an ABP with middle width $w = 5$ ($m = 14$) to compute $\operatorname{perm}_4$, beating the pure combinatorial cut rank $\binom{4}{2} = 6$.

**What Settling $w = 5$ Proves [PROVEN in Regimes 0, 1; CONJECTURE for Regime 2]:**
Settling $w = 5$ proves that **even with arbitrary adjacent-row variable overlap**, commutative cancellation cannot compress the middle layer below the Nisan combinatorial cut rank $\binom{4}{2} = 6$.  
Thus, **Grenet's $m = 15$ construction is strictly optimal** within the entire class of row-separated homogeneous ABPs for $\operatorname{perm}_4$.

### 5.3 Honest Scale Perspective: Finite Result vs Asymptotic VP vs VNP
To maintain mathematical honesty, the council must be explicit about what this result does **not** do:

1. **Finite vs Asymptotic:**
   Settling $w = 5$ for $\operatorname{perm}_4$ is a finite theorem for $n = 4$. Valiant's VP vs VNP conjecture asks whether $\operatorname{dc}(\operatorname{perm}_n) = n^{\omega(1)}$ as $n \to \infty$. A lower bound for $n = 4$ does not by itself prove asymptotic super-polynomial growth.
2. **Row-Separated vs General Determinantal Projections:**
   A general determinantal projection expresses $\operatorname{perm}_n(X) = \det(A_0 + \sum_{i,j=1}^n x_{ij} A_{ij})$ where the $A_{ij}$ are arbitrary constant matrices of size $m \times m$.
   - General determinantal projections permit **arbitrary global linear mixing** across all $n^2$ variables simultaneously.
   - The best known lower bound for general determinantal projections is $m \ge n^2 / 2 + 1$ (Mignon–Ressayre 2004, Cai–Chen–Li 2008), which for $n = 4$ is $m \ge 9$.
   - Grenet's construction gives $m = 2^n - 1$ ($m = 15$ for $n = 4$).
   - The gap between $m = 9$ and $m = 15$ reflects the restriction to row-separated layered ABPs. Proving $m \ge 15$ for row-separated ABPs leaves general non-layered determinantal projections at $m \ge 9$.

### 5.4 Generalization to $n \times n$ Matrices
Can this argument generalize to arbitrary $n$?
- At the middle cut of $\operatorname{perm}_n$, contracting rows $\{1..\lfloor n/2 \rfloor\}$ and $\{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor + 1..n\}$ produces a combinatorial cut rank of $\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor} \sim \frac{2^n}{\sqrt{\pi n / 2}}$.
- If adjacent-layer variable sharing between pairs of rows is blocked by the generalized Lemma C and Westwick-type arguments, then Grenet's middle width $\binom{n}{n/2}$ is strictly uncompressible for all $n$.
- This would establish a formal theorem: **Every row-separated homogeneous ABP for $\operatorname{perm}_n$ requires middle width exactly $\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$.**
- While exponential ($2^{\Omega(n)}$), this bound is specific to the row-separated model and does not transfer to general determinantal complexity without new ideas bridging global variable mixing.
